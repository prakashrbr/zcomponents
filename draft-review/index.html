Reviewing an AI note shouldn't feel like filling in a form

Three directions for the Draft Review component that trade stacked cards for physical, playful metaphors. Each one keeps the safety rules intact: every claim is traceable, missing sources are obvious, and nothing is final until a clinician says so.

  • B. Visit replay
  • C. Pencil to ink

A. Threads

A detective's string board. Every sentence in the note is tied to the words it came from with a thread. Claims with no source have a loose end, swinging free.

Hover or tab through the note. Click a sentence to send a pulse down its thread. Try the two amber sentences.

Threads need width. On phones this direction falls back to the source slip under each sentence.

Why it's more than decoration

The loose thread turns the most dangerous case, a claim with no source, into the most visible thing on screen. Linking claims to sources is the pattern with the strongest evidence behind it. "Cut the thread" and "Tie it off" make resolving a check a single, memorable gesture.

Watch for

  • Gets tangled past about 15 claims per view; filter threads to the hovered section.
  • Needs a wide screen; phones need the fallback.
  • The threads are decorative for screen readers; every link must also exist in text.

B. Visit replay

The visit as a scrubbable recording. As the playhead moves, each sentence of the note appears at the moment it was said. Sentences that were never heard never appear, and parts of the conversation that didn't make it into the note stay hollow on the timeline.

Press play, or drag across the recording. Click any sentence to jump to where it was said. Then find the thing the patient said that never made it into the note.

Why it's more than decoration

It's built for late signing, when the visit is a fading memory: a 20-second replay refreshes it. Hollow marks on the timeline show things said but not written down, which surfaces omissions, the most common scribe error, without a checklist.

Watch for

  • Time order differs from note order; keep the note in note order and only animate arrival.
  • Replay must not become required. It's a mode, not a gate.
  • Audio retention policies may remove the recording; the timeline still works from the transcript.

C. Pencil to ink

The AI writes in pencil. When you check a line, it turns to ink. A second reader has left red-pencil notes in the margin where something looks off. You sign by holding the pen to the line.

Click a grey line to see its source, then ink it. Read the margin notes. Write a line of your own. Hold the signature line to sign.

In ink: 0 of 8 lines

Why it's more than decoration

Pencil and ink make authorship physical: the clinician can see at a glance how much of the page they've vouched for. Margin notes put flags beside the text, not over it, like a colleague reviewing a draft. Hold-to-sign turns signing into a deliberate moment instead of a reflex click.

Watch for

  • Pencil grey must still pass 4.5:1 contrast; unchecked text is still clinical text.
  • Holding is hard for some people, so a click alternative is always shown.
  • "Ink the rest" per section must skip flagged lines, or the metaphor becomes a rubber stamp.

Ideas left out, and why

Swipe to accept, card by card

Fun and fast, which is the problem. It turns acceptance into a reflex and strips each sentence of its context. The evidence on automation bias points the other way.

Points, streaks, and leaderboards

They reward speed. Whether clinicians sign drafts unedited already varies mostly by individual habit; a game layer would push the wrong habit.

Fog over unreviewed text

Blurring what hasn't been checked hides clinical content, hurts accessibility, and removes context needed to judge neighbouring sentences.

Chat with your note as the main review

Useful as a helper, but it puts the burden of knowing what to ask on the clinician, and omissions stay invisible.
